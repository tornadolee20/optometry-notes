# core-models.md

Updated: 2026-04-15

## Purpose

This reference holds the minimum model set behind the consumer behavior psychology framework.

It is intentionally practical rather than academic-exhaustive.

## Core Models

### 1. Loss Aversion

People often react more strongly to possible loss than equivalent gain.

Implication:

- reduce perceived downside
- address regret
- help the customer feel protected from a bad choice

### 2. Choice Overload

Too many options can reduce confidence rather than increase satisfaction.

Implication:

- narrow comparisons
- simplify decision paths
- avoid feature dumping

### 3. Framing Effect

How the same option is described changes how it is perceived.

Implication:

- translate technical attributes into lived consequences
- frame tradeoffs clearly
- do not rely on raw specs alone

### 4. Social Proof And Social Safety

People often look for signals that a choice is normal, accepted, and low-risk.

Implication:

- use real pattern language
- normalize hesitation
- show that uncertainty is common and discussable

### 5. Cognitive Ease

What is easier to understand often feels safer and more trustworthy.

Implication:

- simplify language
- use concrete examples
- reduce comparison fatigue

### 6. Endowment / Identity Protection

People often defend their current choice because changing feels like admitting prior error or risking identity discomfort.

Implication:

- avoid making the customer feel judged
- frame upgrading as fitting current needs, not correcting stupidity

## Practical Rule

In real decision settings, do not ask only:

- "is the product better?"

Also ask:

- "does the customer feel safe enough to choose it?"

## Bottom Line

Consumer behavior often looks irrational from the outside, but inside it is usually risk management, identity management, or uncertainty management.

# friction-patterns.md

Updated: 2026-04-15

## Purpose

This reference helps identify the dominant friction behind hesitation or weak conversion.

Use it to diagnose the main blockage, not to create a giant checklist.

## Core Patterns

### 1. Regret Avoidance

The person fears making a choice they will later blame themselves for.

Typical signals:

- `what if this is not right for me?`
- `let me think about it first`
- repeated comparison loops

Best response direction:

- reduce all-or-nothing feeling
- clarify how the choice was made
- explain what can be adjusted or reviewed later

### 2. Overload Friction

The person is not refusing.

They are overloaded.

Typical signals:

- too many questions
- frozen reaction to multiple options
- asks for "the difference" repeatedly

Best response direction:

- narrow the choice set
- compare only the meaningful differences
- give a recommended path with reason

### 3. Trust Friction

The issue is not the product.

It is whether the person trusts the guidance.

Typical signals:

- price objection arrives before understanding
- resistance rises when recommendation becomes more premium
- person seems to protect themselves from being sold to

Best response direction:

- slow down
- make reasoning visible
- acknowledge tradeoffs honestly

### 4. Abstract Benefit Friction

The benefit sounds real in theory but not vivid in lived experience.

Typical signals:

- `I know, but do I really need it?`
- person understands features but not consequences
- benefit feels optional because it is not pictured concretely

Best response direction:

- translate features into everyday scenes
- make the future discomfort more imaginable
- compare current state and likely lived outcome

### 5. Identity Protection Friction

The person protects self-image, past choices, or role identity.

Typical signals:

- defensiveness
- minimizing the issue
- rejecting the recommendation as "too much"

Best response direction:

- avoid implying they were wrong before
- frame the choice as fit-for-now
- preserve dignity while updating the decision

### 6. Family / Social Friction

The chooser is not the only stakeholder.

Typical signals:

- `I need to ask my family`
- hesitation increases when another person enters
- decision language becomes socially cautious

Best response direction:

- make explanation transferable
- help the person explain the choice to others
- reduce the social risk of endorsing the decision

## Selection Rule

Choose the dominant friction that explains the hesitation best.

Do not diagnose with five patterns when one pattern would guide the next move.

## Bottom Line

Most hesitation is not random.

It is usually a protection strategy in disguise.

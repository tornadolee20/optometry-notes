# 01 Writings

## Core Published Ideas

### 1. Alchemy

- Public framing of the book centers on the claim that many valuable ideas do not emerge from linear logic alone.
- Rory argues that human decisions are shaped by psychology, signaling, status, context, expectation, and perceived meaning.
- The hidden message is not "logic is useless," but that narrow logic systematically misses forms of value that matter in real behavior.

Implications:
- Seemingly irrational customer decisions often become understandable once we look at incentives, emotion, and framing.
- Product, pricing, and communication should be evaluated partly by how they feel, not only by objective performance.
- In many cases, a breakthrough comes from reframing or redesigning the context rather than upgrading the underlying mechanism.

### 2. Life lessons from an ad man

- Rory contrasts economic value with psychological value.
- He argues that organizations over-invest in what is easy to measure and under-invest in what is easy for humans to feel.
- He repeatedly pushes the idea that there are "psycho-logical" solutions that can outperform narrowly logical ones.

Implications:
- A customer experience can improve without any physical change if meaning, expectation, or emotional comfort improves.
- Price resistance is often partly a symptom of weak perceived value, not merely insufficient affordability.
- Small wording changes can unlock behavior that brute-force feature improvement cannot.

### 3. Perspective is everything

- This talk focuses on reframing as a real, material intervention in human experience.
- Rory suggests that the circumstances of life can matter less than the interpretation attached to them.
- He treats perception as an active design variable rather than a passive afterthought.

Implications:
- Explanations do not merely decorate reality; they shape how reality is experienced.
- Better pre-framing can reduce dissatisfaction, impatience, and regret.
- In optometry, adaptation discomfort can feel dramatically different when it has meaning and a time horizon.

### 4. Sweat the small stuff

- Rory treats minor details as potentially high-leverage behavioral triggers.
- He rejects the managerial instinct to ignore small variables because they look trivial in spreadsheets.
- His broader point is that many large systems fail to notice where humans are actually making judgments.

Implications:
- "Tiny" service design details may determine trust more than large behind-the-scenes investments.
- Follow-up wording, tone of handoff, delay explanations, reminder sequence, and trial rituals all matter.
- Human decisions are sensitive to context in a way operational models often underestimate.

## Recurring Written Themes

### Theme 1: The tyranny of objective metrics

- What gets measured often gets overvalued.
- What matters to people is often indirect, emotional, or situational.
- Organizations become blind when they trust dashboards more than lived experience.

### Theme 2: Cheap contextual changes can outperform expensive structural changes

- Rory often highlights low-cost improvements with unexpectedly large effects.
- He values elegant, asymmetric interventions.
- He likes ideas that exploit human psychology rather than fight it.

### Theme 3: Human beings respond to meaning, not only mechanics

- The same waiting time, friction, or inconvenience can feel very different depending on narrative and framing.
- Explanation can create emotional utility.
- Ceremony and ritual are not fluff when they alter confidence and action.

## Practical Reading for Uncle Glasses

Read Rory as a writer who gives permission to take customer psychology seriously without becoming manipulative.

What to borrow:
- respect for perceived value
- willingness to question "rational" default assumptions
- love of low-cost, high-leverage interventions
- attention to status, reassurance, and expectation design

What not to borrow blindly:
- any move that weakens clinical accuracy
- any framing that exaggerates outcomes
- any cleverness that makes trust feel gimmicky
